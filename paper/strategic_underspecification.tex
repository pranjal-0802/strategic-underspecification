\begin{abstract}
Users of coding assistants routinely under-specify their intent, and the assistant's policy for handling missing information (whether guessing silently or asking a clarifying question) is an endogenous strategic choice that users respond to. We develop a contract-theoretic model of endogenous clarification in human--AI interaction: strategic under-specification is not driven by models becoming better at guessing; it emerges because users strategically economize on specification effort in response to the assistant's asking policy when the assistant's objective diverges from true user welfare. Formalizing this interaction as a Stackelberg game between an assistant (leader) and a population of users with privately known specification costs (followers), we first establish a decision-theoretic benchmark with an exogenous policy. By the envelope theorem, improving raw guessing accuracy alone weakly improves user welfare despite shortening prompts, showing that informal claims that better guessing degrades outcomes conflate technological improvement with policy misalignment.

Closing the loop, we solve for the assistant's optimal policy under two governance regimes: a \emph{pooling} policy restricted to a single population-wide ask rate, and a \emph{screening} policy offering an incentive-compatible menu conditioned on prompt specification effort. We show that an unbiased (welfare-aligned) assistant achieves first-best efficiency under screening despite users' private cost information, as incentive compatibility is costless when principal and agent share an objective. Consequently, all screening-regime distortion is attributable to misalignment between the assistant's objective and true user welfare, not to asymmetric information per se. Under pooling on the physically feasible domain ($m \le k$), an unbiased assistant sets the full asking rate, whereas perceived asking friction weakly suppresses clarification turns ($\partial a^{SE}/\partial \lambda_A \le 0$ on the interior domain). When the assistant is biased, screening induces downward distortion in the asking service offered to high-specification-cost users to prevent low-cost users from mimicking. We characterize both equilibria under a tractable linear-risk specification, evaluate robustness under the exact nonlinear conjunctive specification, and outline a proposed human-subject experimental design.

\section{Introduction}

A user typing a coding request faces a fundamental economic trade-off: spend cognitive effort articulating edge cases, naming conventions, and constraints, or leave them implicit and trust the assistant to infer them. The assistant, in turn, must decide for each unspecified detail whether to guess or to ask. Neither choice is made in a vacuum: the user's specification effort depends on what they expect the assistant to do with silence, and the assistant's ask/guess policy is chosen (explicitly, or as an emergent property of training and product incentives) with some implicit model of how users will respond.

This paper provides a mechanism-design theory of endogenous clarification in AI assistance. Its central premise is that strategic under-specification is not caused by AI getting better at guessing; it is caused by users responding to the assistant's endogenous clarification policy when the assistant's objective differs from theirs. Section~\ref{sec:baseline} begins with a decision-theoretic benchmark where the assistant's policy is exogenous, proving that improved guessing accuracy alone weakly improves user welfare despite shortening prompts. Section~\ref{sec:sources} identifies the true moral-hazard channel: unobserved divergence between the user's belief and the assistant's true asking policy. Sections~\ref{sec:stackelberg}--\ref{sec:compare} close the loop by modeling the assistant as a Stackelberg leader anticipating user best responses under pooling and screening instruments. Section~\ref{sec:predictions} translates the equilibrium characterizations into eight falsifiable behavioral predictions (P1, P1$'$, P2--P7). Section~\ref{sec:discussion} evaluates exact nonlinear robustness and discusses institutional extensions.

\subsection{Related literature}

This work bridges contract theory and the emerging literature on formal models of human--AI interaction.

\paragraph{Contract theory and screening without transfers.}
Our screening framework builds on classical adverse-selection models~\citep{myerson1981,baron-myerson1982,mussa-rosen1978,laffont-tirole1986} that exploit the Spence--Mirrlees single-crossing property~\citep{spence1973} to separate heterogeneous agents. However, unlike classical monopoly or regulatory screening where the principal uses monetary transfers to extract information rents, here the assistant screens strictly through an interactive service margin (the ask rate $a$ offered in response to specification effort $m$). This connects to non-monetary screening and costly verification~\citep{townsend1979}, where information revelation is governed by operational friction rather than cash transfers.

\paragraph{Delegation, authority, and information design.}
Our analysis relates closely to the theory of delegation under asymmetric information~\citep{holmstrom1984}, where a principal determines how much discretion to grant an agent, and to models of formal versus real authority~\citep{aghion-tirole1997}, where an agent's upstream effort is shaped by who retains authority over downstream implementation. In our setting, leaving attributes unspecified implicitly delegates their resolution to the assistant. Furthermore, because the assistant's asking policy determines which state dimensions are actively resolved, our framework shares conceptual foundations with Bayesian persuasion and strategic information design~\citep{kamenica-gentzkow2011}, while focusing on the moral-hazard trade-off in prompt articulation.

\paragraph{Costly communication and strategic disclosure.}
The user's specification decision formalizes costly disclosure under strategic interpretation, connecting to strategic information transmission~\citep{crawford-sobel1982}, voluntary quality disclosure~\citep{grossman1981}, and rational inattention~\citep{sims2003}. In our setting, under-specification arises not from an incentive to deceive, but from the user's rational economizing on specification costs when anticipating automated assistance.

\paragraph{Assistance games and human--AI interaction.}
Our bi-level formulation models the interaction as a Stackelberg game~\citep{stackelberg1934}, connecting conceptually to the literature on assistance games and cooperative inverse reinforcement learning~\citep{hadfield-menell2016}, where an autonomous assistant acts to maximize human utility under incomplete information. However, while assistance games formalize the assistant's uncertainty over the human's underlying \emph{objective or reward function}, in our setting the economic task objective is known and shared (downstream success value $V$ and error loss $L$). Instead, the informational asymmetry resides entirely in the user's private \emph{specification cost} $\kappa$. The strategic margin in our model is therefore not reward learning or preference alignment, but rather the contract-theoretic trade-off between prompt specification effort and automated clarification under costly communication. Our model provides a contract-theoretic microfoundation for this ask-versus-guess margin under asymmetric specification costs.

\paragraph{Clarification and interactive NLP.}
A parallel empirical literature in natural language processing and information retrieval investigates algorithms for generating and ranking clarifying questions in conversational search and dialog~\citep{rao-daume2018,aliannejadi2019,zamani2020}. These works optimize static clarification decisions or train heuristic dialog policies via reinforcement learning against fixed datasets. However, these approaches typically treat user inputs as exogenous benchmark queries or fixed dialog instances rather than the endogenous choice of an optimizing agent who strategically anticipates the assistant's clarification policy. Our framework provides the contract-theoretic foundation that this engineering literature lacks, formally endogenizing prompt specification effort as an equilibrium best response to the assistant's asking policy.

\section{Environment and the Decision-Theoretic Benchmark}
\label{sec:baseline}

\subsection{Setup}

A task has $k$ binary, independent, intent-relevant attributes
$\theta = (\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k) \in \{0,1\}^k$, each drawn i.i.d.\ uniform and
privately known to the user. The user chooses a specification effort level
$m \in \{0,\dots,k\}$ and truthfully reveals $m$ attributes.\footnote{We assume
truthful revelation conditional on the choice to specify: the strategic margin is
\emph{how much} to say, not whether to lie about what is said. A cheap-talk layer
on top of this costly-specification layer is a natural extension; see
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.} Specifying $m$ attributes costs $c(m)$, with
$c(0)=0$, $c' \ge 0$, $c'' \ge 0$.

For each of the $k-m$ unspecified attributes, the assistant either \emph{asks}
(revealing the true value, at a cost to the user) or \emph{guesses}, succeeding
independently with probability $g \in (0,1)$. We model the assistant's policy at
this stage as a uniform ask rate $a \in [0,1]$ applied to every unspecified
attribute, so each is correctly resolved with probability
$q(a,g) = a + (1-a)g$. Task success requires \emph{all} $k$ attributes to be
correctly resolved (conjunctive: one wrong parameter or convention typically breaks
the deliverable). The user's payoff is
\[
U(m; a, g) \;=\; V \, q(a,g)^{\,k-m} \;-\; c(m) \;-\; a\,c_Q\,(k-m),
\]
with $V>0$ the value of success and $c_Q>0$ the user's cognitive cost of answering one
clarifying question. We treat $m$ as continuous on $[0,k]$ for tractability.

\subsection{Proposition 1: accuracy alone does not create moral hazard}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:accuracy}
Fix $a$. Let $U^*(g) = \max_{m \in [0,k]} U(m;a,g)$. Then $U^*$ is weakly
increasing in $g$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the envelope theorem, at the optimum $m^*(g)$,
$dU^*/dg = \partial U/\partial g\big|_{m=m^*(g)} = V(k-m^*)q^{k-m^*-1}(1-a) \ge 0$.
The envelope theorem lets us ignore $dm^*/dg$: because $m^*$ is chosen optimally,
its first-order response to $g$ has no first-order effect on $U^*$.
\end{proof}

\begin{assumption}[Regularity]\label{ass:reg}
In the relevant range of $m$, $(k-m)\ln q(a,g) \ge -1$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{remark}[Interpreting Assumption~\ref{ass:reg} and Model Concavity]
\label{rmk:assumption1-audit}
The regularity condition $(k-m)\ln q(a,g) \ge -1$ is a sufficient condition for the single-crossing property used in Corollary~\ref{cor:mono} via Topkis's monotone comparative statics theorem~\citep{topkis1978,topkis1998,milgrom-shannon1994}. The direction is pinned down by the cross-partial of utility: for fixed $a$,
\[
\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial m\,\partial g} \;=\; -V(1-a)\,q^{\,k-m-1}\Big[(k-m)\ln q + 1\Big].
\]
Thus $U$ exhibits \emph{decreasing differences} in $(m,g)$, which Topkis's theorem requires to guarantee that $m^*(g)$ is weakly decreasing, if and only if $(k-m)\ln q + 1 \ge 0$, which is precisely Assumption~\ref{ass:reg}. Since $\ln q \approx -(1-q)$ for $q$ near 1, this condition requires $(k-m)(1-q) \lesssim 1$: the expected number of residual unresolved attributes under guessing is at most about one. If this condition fails ($(k-m)\ln q < -1$), $U$ exhibits increasing differences, and higher guessing accuracy can locally induce higher specification effort before falling as $q \to 1$.

We also note that in the exact conjunctive model, the gross benefit $V q^{k-m}$ is strictly convex in $m$ (since $\partial^2 [V q^{k-m}]/\partial m^2 = V q^{k-m}(\ln q)^2 > 0$). Global concavity of $U$ in $m$ therefore requires the specification cost curvature $c''(m) = \kappa$ to strictly exceed $V q^{k-m}(\ln q)^2$ across $[0, k]$. Under the linear-risk specification introduced in Section~\ref{sec:stackelberg}, by contrast, gross benefit is linear in $m$, guaranteeing that $U$ is globally strictly concave in $m$ for all $\kappa > 0$.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:mono}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:reg}, $m^*(g)$ is weakly decreasing in $g$ (decreasing
differences in $(m,g)$; Topkis's theorem).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Immediate from the cross-partial computed in Remark~\ref{rmk:assumption1-audit}:
Assumption~\ref{ass:reg} is exactly the condition $\partial^2U/\partial m\,\partial g \le 0$
on the relevant range, which is decreasing differences in $(m,g)$; Topkis's
theorem then gives $m^*(g)$ weakly decreasing.
\end{proof}

Thus, holding the assistant's policy fixed, users specify less as the assistant becomes
more adept at guessing, yet they are never worse off for doing so.
Proposition~\ref{prop:accuracy} serves primarily as a baseline: it applies the
envelope theorem to a single agent optimizing against a technology
parameter that enters the payoff with a non-negative derivative, a property that holds
generally in models of this class. Its purpose is not to present a surprising
result, but to establish the rigorous benchmark against which claimed moral hazard
must be evaluated, ensuring that Proposition~\ref{prop:bias} isolates the
true source of welfare loss. This frames the central question of this paper:
what happens once the policy $a$ is no longer exogenous, but chosen by a strategic
assistant that anticipates the user's response?

\section{Sources of Strategic Under-Specification}
\label{sec:sources}

Proposition~\ref{prop:accuracy} assumed the assistant's ask rate is fixed and that
users correctly anticipate $q(a,g)$. Both assumptions can fail.

\subsection{Cost externality}
If a third party (such as a code reviewer or downstream production system) bears part of the
cost of an erroneous guess, the user's private parameters $(\kappa, V)$ understate the social
costs, causing the user to under-specify relative to the social optimum at any fixed
$g$, an inefficiency that worsens as $g$ rises by Corollary~\ref{cor:mono}. While this constitutes
a standard Pigouvian externality, our primary focus lies in strategic and informational friction.

\subsection{Miscalibrated belief updating under silent failure}
If incorrect guesses are not always detected, and the detection probability $d(m)$ is
increasing in $m$, users infer $g$ from experience rather than observing it directly.
Under-specifying users are consequently less likely to catch their own errors, generating an
upward-biased estimate $\hat g$ that reinforces under-specification. This mechanism represents
a dynamic learning process rather than a static equilibrium outcome; we formalize its testable
implications in Section~\ref{sec:predictions} (prediction P5).

\subsection{Belief about the assistant's policy and equilibrium under-specification}
\label{sec:naive-soph}
When the clarification policy $a$ is endogenous, the user's specification decision depends on their belief regarding the assistant's asking rate. Let users be \emph{sophisticated} (correctly inferring the assistant's true operational ask rate $a_A$) or \emph{naive} (believing the assistant follows a higher clarification rate $a^\dagger > a_A$, such as the unconstrained welfare-optimal policy).

\begin{definition}[Strategic Under-Specification]
\label{def:underspec}
A user exhibits \emph{strategic under-specification} if their chosen prompt specification effort $m$ is strictly lower than the full-information, correctly calibrated benchmark:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item Relative to sophisticated belief calibration: $m^*_{\text{naive}} < m^*_{\text{soph}}$; or
\item Relative to unassisted specification: $m^*(a) < m^*(0)$ under automated assistance.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

In human--AI interaction, varying the clarification rate $a$ engages two opposing economic channels:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{The accuracy channel:} A higher ask rate increases effective resolution accuracy $q(a,g) = a + (1-a)g$. Under decreasing differences (Assumption~\ref{ass:reg}), greater accuracy reduces the marginal value of upstream prompt effort, pushing specification downward.
\item \emph{The question-cost channel:} Each clarifying question imposes an interactive answering cost $c_Q$ on the user. Because the total answering cost $a c_Q (k-m)$ is decreasing in specification $m$, a higher ask rate increases the marginal penalty of leaving attributes unspecified, pushing specification upward (pre-emption).
\end{enumerate}

Under the linear-risk specification analyzed in Sections~\ref{sec:stackelberg}--\ref{sec:model2}, where the net benefit of clarification $s \equiv \Lambda - c_Q > 0$ is positive, the cross-partial derivative of user utility is:
\[
\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial m\,\partial a} \;=\; -(\Lambda - c_Q) \;<\; 0.
\]
The accuracy channel strictly dominates the question-cost channel, ensuring that $U$ exhibits strictly decreasing differences in $(m,a)$.

\begin{proposition}[Equilibrium Under-Specification under Naive Beliefs]\label{prop:bias}
Under the linear-risk specification with $\Lambda > c_Q$, naive users choose weakly lower specification effort than sophisticated users:
\[
m^*_{\text{naive}} \;\le\; m^*_{\text{soph}},
\]
holding strictly ($m^*_{\text{naive}} < m^*_{\text{soph}}$) for all interior types satisfying $\kappa > \frac{\Lambda(1-a^\dagger) + a^\dagger c_Q}{k}$. Consequently, naive users achieve weakly lower true welfare, $U(m^*_{\text{naive}};a_A) \le U(m^*_{\text{soph}};a_A)$, strictly so on the interior domain. Holding raw guessing accuracy $g$ fixed, naive users strategically under-specify and suffer an equilibrium welfare loss.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Under linear risk on the physical domain $m \in [0, k]$, the follower's best response to an anticipated ask rate $a$ is $m^*(\kappa;a) = \min\{k, \beta(\kappa;a)\}$, where $\beta(\kappa;a) \equiv \frac{\Lambda(1-a) + a c_Q}{\kappa}$. Since $\Lambda > c_Q$, the unconstrained response $\beta(\kappa;a)$ is strictly decreasing in $a$: $\partial\beta/\partial a = (c_Q - \Lambda)/\kappa < 0$. A naive user anticipating $a^\dagger > a_A$ therefore satisfies $\beta(\kappa;a^\dagger) < \beta(\kappa;a_A)$.
For low-cost types with $\kappa \le \frac{\Lambda(1-a^\dagger) + a^\dagger c_Q}{k}$, we have $\beta(\kappa;a^\dagger) \ge k$, so $\beta(\kappa;a_A) > k$ as well. Both beliefs therefore yield boundary saturation: $m^*_{\text{naive}} = m^*_{\text{soph}} = k$, and true welfare is identical ($U(m^*_{\text{naive}};a_A) = U(m^*_{\text{soph}};a_A)$).
For all interior types satisfying $\kappa > \frac{\Lambda(1-a^\dagger) + a^\dagger c_Q}{k}$, we have $\beta(\kappa;a^\dagger) < k$, which guarantees:
\[
m^*_{\text{naive}} \;=\; \beta(\kappa;a^\dagger) \;<\; \min\{k, \beta(\kappa;a_A)\} \;=\; m^*_{\text{soph}}.
\]
Because true utility $U(m;a_A)$ is strictly concave in $m$ with unique global maximizer on $[0, k]$ at $m^*_{\text{soph}}$, any choice $m \ne m^*_{\text{soph}}$ incurs a strict utility loss. On the fully interior domain where $m^*_{\text{soph}} = \beta(\kappa;a_A) < k$, evaluating the quadratic shortfall directly yields:
\[
U(m^*_{\text{soph}};a_A) - U(m^*_{\text{naive}};a_A) \;=\; \frac{\kappa}{2}\big(m^*_{\text{soph}} - m^*_{\text{naive}}\big)^2 \;=\; \frac{(\Lambda - c_Q)^2}{2\kappa}\,(a^\dagger - a_A)^2 \;>\; 0,
\]
confirming that interior naive users are strictly worse off.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Exact Model Counterexample to Monotone Under-Specification]
\label{rmk:prop2-counterexample}
In the exact conjunctive model $U(m,a) = V q(a,g)^{k-m} - c(m) - a c_Q (k-m)$, the cross-partial derivative is:
\[
\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial m\,\partial a} \;=\; -V(1-g)\,q^{k-m-1}\big[(k-m)\ln q + 1\big] \;+\; c_Q.
\]
Even when Assumption~\ref{ass:reg} holds, the question-answering cost term $+c_Q$ can dominate if questions are costly relative to baseline task value $V$. For example, with $k=5, V=10, \kappa=0.5, g=0.8, c_Q=2, a_A=0.1$, and $a^\dagger=0.6$, Assumption~\ref{ass:reg} holds across the domain, yet sophisticated users choose $m^*_{\text{soph}} = 3.15$ while naive users choose $m^*_{\text{naive}} = 3.92$. Here, the naive user anticipates substantial clarification costs and over-specifies upstream to avoid conversational interruptions. Proposition~\ref{prop:bias} thus isolates the regime where clarification is socially cost-effective ($\Lambda > c_Q$), which is the relevant environment for helpful automated assistants.
\end{remark}

This identifies the first true source of moral hazard: not technological accuracy, but
an unobserved gap between the user's belief regarding the assistant's policy and
the assistant's actual policy. Section~\ref{sec:model1} solves for the optimal
policy $a_A$ when the assistant acts as a strategic, potentially biased
Stackelberg leader, allowing Proposition~\ref{prop:bias} to be evaluated with an
endogenously derived policy rather than an exogenously assumed one.

\section{The Assistant's Policy as a Stackelberg Game}
\label{sec:stackelberg}

\subsection{General formulation}

The assistant (leader) commits ex ante to a policy $\pi$ before meeting any user.
A population of users (followers) has privately known specification-cost type
$\kappa \sim F$ on $[\kappa_L,\kappa_H]$, with cost $c(m;\kappa)=\kappa m^2/2$, and
best-responds $m^*(\kappa;\pi) = \arg\max_m U(m;\pi,\kappa)$. The leader's own
objective $\Pi$ need not equal $U$: we write
\[
\Pi_\kappa(m,a) \;=\; \mu_A\, U(m,a;\kappa) \;-\; (\lambda_A - c_Q)\, a\,(k-m),
\]
where $\mu_A \in (0,1]$ discounts the leader's weight on true downstream user welfare, $c_Q$ is the user's true cognitive cost of answering one clarifying question, and $\lambda_A$ is the leader's own perceived or institutional cost per clarifying question. In commercial AI systems, misalignment along these margins arises from concrete engineering and economic forces:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Asking friction bias} ($\lambda_A > c_Q$): Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and preference optimization algorithms (e.g., DPO) commonly evaluate conversation turns in isolation. Reward models systematically penalize ``evasive'' clarifying questions in favor of immediate code generation because users in rating pools favor direct answers. Furthermore, multi-turn interactions increase token costs, context-window expansion, and inference latency, creating provider-side pressure to suppress clarification turns.
\item \emph{Downstream welfare discounting} ($\mu_A < 1$): Turn-level user satisfaction signals (such as prompt acceptance or immediate positive feedback) capture immediate session fluency. In contrast, the true economic loss $L$ from broken conventions, subtle edge-case errors, or security defects is typically discovered hours or days later in downstream execution, test suites, or code review. Consequently, training objectives tuned to immediate session retention tend to discount downstream correctness.
\end{enumerate}
When asking friction is aligned ($\lambda_A = c_Q$), the leader's objective satisfies $\Pi_\kappa \equiv \mu_A U(\cdot;\kappa)$, sharing the identical preferences and policy maximizers as true user welfare for any positive welfare weight $\mu_A > 0$. Downstream welfare discounting $\mu_A < 1$ creates policy distortion only through interaction with excess asking friction ($\lambda_A \ne c_Q$), which inflates the effective asking friction per unit of altruism $(\lambda_A - c_Q)/\mu_A$. The leader solves $\pi^* = \arg\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{\kappa\sim F}[\Pi_\kappa(m^*(\kappa;\pi),\pi)]$, a bilevel program.

\begin{remark}[Existence]
For Model I below ($\pi=a\in[0,1]$, a compact scalar policy), $m^*(\kappa;a)$ is
continuous in $a$ (strict concavity of $U$ in $m$ plus the implicit function
theorem), so $\mathbb{E}_\kappa[\Pi_\kappa]$ is continuous in $a$ and a maximizer
exists on $[0,1]$ by Weierstrass's theorem. For Model II below (a finite menu over
two types), the feasible set is a non-empty, compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^4$ cut out by
continuous (bilinear in $(m,a)$ and quadratic in $m$) IC/IR constraints on the compact domain $[0,k]^2 \times [0,1]^2$. The constraint set is non-empty because the degenerate pooling allocation $(m^*(a), a, m^*(a), a)$ satisfies all constraints. By Weierstrass's theorem, the continuous leader objective attains a global maximum; no ironing or ex-ante randomization is needed with only
two types.
\end{remark}

For tractability in what follows we replace the exact conjunctive success
probability $q^{k-m}$ with its first-order (small-risk) linear approximation around
$q\to 1$: expected loss $\approx L(1-q)(k-m)$. When linearizing total task value $V q^{k-m} \approx V - V(1-q)(k-m)$, the per-attribute loss parameter satisfies $L = V$; more generally, $L \le V$ parameterizes settings where individual unresolved attributes cause localized defect losses without necessarily destroying the entire project value. With $\Lambda \equiv L(1-g)$,
the user's payoff becomes
\[
U(m,a;\kappa) \;=\; V \;-\; \Lambda(1-a)(k-m) \;-\; \frac{\kappa}{2}m^2 \;-\; a\,c_Q\,(k-m).
\]
Throughout Sections~\ref{sec:firstbest}--\ref{sec:model2}, we maintain the physical feasibility constraint $m \in [0, k]$, so that followers cannot specify more attributes than exist in the task. When unconstrained first-order conditions yield $m^*(\kappa;a) = \frac{\Lambda(1-a) + ac_Q}{\kappa} > k$ (which occurs for low-cost types with $\kappa < \Lambda/k$), best-response effort saturates at the physical boundary $m^* = k$.

\subsection{The first-best benchmark and physical feasibility}
\label{sec:firstbest}

Before analyzing either strategic regime, we characterize the fully-informed, fully-aligned social optimum:
\[
(m^{FB}(\kappa), a^{FB}(\kappa)) \;=\; \arg\max_{m\in[0,k],\,a\in[0,1]} U(m,a;\kappa).
\]

\begin{proposition}[First-Best Policy on the Feasible Domain]\label{prop:firstbest}
On the physically feasible domain $m \in [0, k]$, under the linear-risk specification with $\Lambda > c_Q$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item Asking weakly dominates guessing for every cost type $\kappa > 0$: $a^{FB}(\kappa) = 1$, with optimal specification $m^{FB}(\kappa) = \min\{k, c_Q/\kappa\}$.
\item For any type with $\kappa > c_Q/k$, asking strictly dominates guessing: $U(m^*(1), 1) > U(m^*(0), 0)$. For boundary types with $\kappa \le c_Q/k$, specification effort saturates at $m^{FB} = k$, leaving zero residual attributes ($k-m=0$) and rendering the asking policy payoff-irrelevant.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For any fixed specification effort $m \in [0, k]$, the partial derivative of utility with respect to $a$ is:
\[
\frac{\partial U}{\partial a} \;=\; (\Lambda - c_Q)(k - m).
\]
Because $\Lambda > c_Q$, this derivative is strictly positive for all $m < k$ and zero at $m = k$. 
Now consider the joint optimization over $m \in [0, k]$ and $a \in \{0, 1\}$.
Under $a = 1$, the user chooses $m^*(1) = \min\{k, c_Q/\kappa\}$.
Under $a = 0$, the user chooses $m^*(0) = \min\{k, \Lambda/\kappa\}$.
We evaluate the three type intervals:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item If $\kappa \ge \Lambda/k$, both best responses remain interior: $m^*(1) = c_Q/\kappa \le m^*(0) = \Lambda/\kappa \le k$. Evaluating the welfare difference:
\[
U(m^*(1), 1; \kappa) - U(m^*(0), 0; \kappa) \;=\; (\Lambda - c_Q)\left( k - \frac{\Lambda + c_Q}{2\kappa} \right).
\]
Since $\kappa \ge \Lambda/k > \frac{\Lambda + c_Q}{2k}$, the bracketed term satisfies $k - \frac{\Lambda + c_Q}{2\kappa} > k - k = 0$. Hence $U(m^*(1), 1; \kappa) > U(m^*(0), 0; \kappa)$ strictly.
\item If $c_Q/k < \kappa < \Lambda/k$, unconstrained effort under $a = 0$ exceeds $k$, so specification under guessing saturates at the boundary $m^*(0) = k$, yielding $U(k, 0; \kappa) = V - \frac{\kappa}{2}k^2$. Under asking, effort remains interior at $m^*(1) = c_Q/\kappa < k$, yielding $U(m^*(1), 1; \kappa) = V - c_Q k + \frac{c_Q^2}{2\kappa}$. Subtracting:
\[
U(m^*(1), 1; \kappa) - U(k, 0; \kappa) \;=\; \frac{\kappa k^2}{2} - c_Q k + \frac{c_Q^2}{2\kappa} \;=\; \frac{(\kappa k - c_Q)^2}{2\kappa} \;>\; 0.
\]
\item If $\kappa \le c_Q/k$, specification effort saturates at $m^* = k$ under both policies, so $U(k, 1; \kappa) = U(k, 0; \kappa) = V - \frac{\kappa}{2}k^2$.
\end{itemize}
Therefore, $a^{FB}(\kappa) = 1$ is globally optimal across all types $\kappa > 0$, with indifference only on the boundary set $\kappa \le c_Q/k$ where $m^{FB} = k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The Unclipped Threshold $\kappa^*$ and Genuine Asking--Guessing Trade-offs]
\label{rmk:kappa-star}
In unconstrained algebra without the feasibility constraint $m \le k$, setting the difference $(\Lambda - c_Q)(k - \frac{\Lambda + c_Q}{2\kappa})$ to zero yields the formal threshold $\kappa^* \equiv \frac{\Lambda + c_Q}{2k}$. However, since $\Lambda > c_Q$, we have $\kappa^* < \Lambda/k$ strictly. The region $\kappa < \kappa^*$ where guessing formally appeared to beat asking in unclipped algebra lies entirely in the unphysical domain where unconstrained effort $\Lambda/\kappa$ exceeds $k$ (the user would specify more attributes than exist in the task). Once $m \le k$ is enforced, asking weakly dominates guessing everywhere under linear risk because asking resolves uncertainty at lower unit cost ($c_Q < \Lambda$) while allowing the user to economize on specification effort.

Genuine interior thresholds where $a^{FB} \in \{0, 1\}$ strictly partitions the type space arise from two natural richer specifications:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Exact conjunctive success:} Under $V q(a,g)^{k-m} - c(m) - a c_Q (k-m)$, the marginal gain from clarification is $(k-m)[V(1-g)q^{k-m-1} - c_Q]$. When baseline accuracy $g$ is high or $k-m$ is small, this marginal gain turns negative, making guessing strictly optimal for low-cost types who specify most attributes upstream.
\item \emph{Heterogeneous attribute stakes:} If attributes vary in importance with stakes $\Lambda_i$, efficiency prescribes asking only on high-stakes attributes ($\Lambda_i > c_Q$) and guessing on low-stakes attributes ($\Lambda_i < c_Q$).
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\section{Model I: A Pooling (Scalar) Policy}
\label{sec:model1}

The leader is restricted to a single population-wide ask rate $a\in[0,1]$ and
cannot condition on user-specific attributes or observed effort. This provides
a natural benchmark for policies embedded uniformly at training time or applied
without user-level profiling.

\subsection{User best response}

The FOC $\partial U/\partial m = \Lambda(1-a) - \kappa m + ac_Q = 0$ gives, with
$\beta(\kappa)=\Lambda/\kappa$ and $\gamma(\kappa)=(\Lambda-c_Q)/\kappa$,
\[
m^*(\kappa;a) \;=\; \beta(\kappa) - a\,\gamma(\kappa), \qquad
\frac{\partial m^*}{\partial a} \;=\; -\gamma(\kappa) \;=\; \frac{c_Q-\Lambda}{\kappa}.
\]
We assume interior specification levels $m^* \in (0, k)$; for low-cost types where unconstrained $\beta(\kappa) \ge k$, specification effort saturates at $m^* = k$. The closed-form population moments $\bar R_0$ and $\bar\gamma$ below are exact when best responses remain interior, and serve as tractable linear approximations when boundary clipping binds; as verified in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} and our computational robustness suite, the qualitative regime structure and corner-solution properties are strictly preserved under boundary clipping and exact payoffs alike.

\begin{remark}[Non-monotone effect of ask rate on specification]
Unlike Corollary~\ref{cor:mono}, the sign of $\partial m^*/\partial a$ here does
not depend on type: it is positive for \emph{every} type iff $c_Q > \Lambda$, i.e.,
iff answering a question costs users more than the expected loss it avoids. When
that condition holds, a higher population ask rate induces users to specify \emph{more}, not
less; they pre-empt costly clarifying questions by articulating the answer directly.
This introduces a new economic force relative to Section~\ref{sec:baseline},
where $a$ operated solely through effective accuracy, and generates a distinct,
directly testable prediction (prediction P1$'$ below).
\end{remark}

\subsection{The leader's problem}

Let $R(a,\kappa)=k-m^*(\kappa;a)$ denote the residual unspecified attributes. In the pooling regime, the assistant commits ex ante to an operational ask rate $a \in [0,1]$ applied population-wide, without observing or directly contracting on individual specification effort.

Evaluating the general leader objective from Section~\ref{sec:firstbest} at the follower's optimal best response $m^*(\kappa;a) = \beta(\kappa) - a\,\gamma(\kappa)$:
\[
\Pi_\kappa(m^*(\kappa;a),a) \;=\; \mu_A\, U(m^*(\kappa;a),a;\kappa) \;-\; (\lambda_A - c_Q)\, a\,\big(k - m^*(\kappa;a)\big).
\]
Substituting $U(m^*(\kappa;a),a;\kappa) = V - \Lambda(1-a)(k-m^*) - \frac{\kappa}{2}(m^*)^2 - a\,c_Q\,(k-m^*)$, using $k - m^*(\kappa;a) = R_0(\kappa) + a\,\gamma(\kappa)$, and defining the effective resolution stake $s \equiv \Lambda - c_Q$ and the assistant's excess asking friction $b \equiv \lambda_A - c_Q$, expanding in powers of $a$ yields:
\[
\Pi_\kappa(m^*(\kappa;a),a) \;=\; \mu_A \left( V - \Lambda k + \frac{\Lambda^2}{2\kappa} \right) \;+\; (\mu_A s - b)\, R_0(\kappa)\, a \;+\; \frac{(\mu_A s - 2b)\,\gamma(\kappa)}{2}\, a^2.
\]
Averaging over $\kappa \sim F$ requires no moments beyond the population sufficient statistics already defined, $\bar R_0 = k - \mathbb{E}[\beta]$ and $\bar\gamma = \mathbb{E}[\gamma] = s\,\mathbb{E}[1/\kappa]$:
\[
\Pi(a) \;=\; \text{const} \;+\; \bar L\, a \;+\; \bar Q\, a^2,
\]
where $\text{const} = \mu_A(V - \Lambda k) + \frac{\mu_A \Lambda^2}{2}\mathbb{E}[1/\kappa]$, and
\[
\bar L \;\equiv\; (\mu_A s - b)\,\bar R_0, \qquad \bar Q \;\equiv\; \frac{(\mu_A s - 2b)\,\bar\gamma}{2}.
\]

\begin{proposition}[Stackelberg-optimal pooling rate]\label{prop:aSE}
Provided $\bar Q < 0$ (the second-order condition for strict concavity, equivalent to $2(\lambda_A - c_Q) > \mu_A(\Lambda - c_Q)$), the leader's unconstrained stationary point is
\[
a^{SE} \;=\; -\,\frac{\bar L}{2\bar Q} \;=\; -\,\frac{(\mu_A s - b)\,\bar R_0}{(\mu_A s - 2b)\,\bar\gamma}.
\]
When this stationary point lies in $[0,1]$, it is the unique global maximum of $\Pi(a)$ on $[0,1]$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}[Friction bias suppresses the pooling ask rate on the interior branch]
\label{cor:bias-direction}
On the interior branch $\bar Q < 0$, differentiating the closed form in Proposition~\ref{prop:aSE} directly with respect to $\lambda_A$ (holding primitives $\mu_A,\Lambda,c_Q,\bar R_0,\bar\gamma$ fixed) gives:
\[
\frac{\partial a^{SE}}{\partial \lambda_A} \;=\; -\,\frac{\bar R_0}{\bar\gamma}\,\frac{\mu_A s}{(\mu_A s - 2b)^2} \;=\; -\,\frac{\bar R_0}{\bar\gamma}\,\frac{\mu_A (\Lambda - c_Q)}{\big[\mu_A (\Lambda - c_Q) - 2(\lambda_A - c_Q)\big]^2}.
\]
For populations supported on the interior specification domain ($\kappa \ge \Lambda/k$ for all types in the support), follower effort satisfies $m^*(\kappa; 0) = \Lambda/\kappa \le k$, ensuring that expected residual unspecified attributes under silence satisfy $\bar R_0 = k - \mathbb{E}[\beta] > 0$. Because $\bar\gamma > 0$, $\mu_A > 0$, and $s > 0$, it follows that:
\[
\frac{\partial a^{SE}}{\partial \lambda_A} \;\le\; 0.
\]
On this interior domain, higher perceived clarification friction $\lambda_A$ strictly suppresses the assistant's equilibrium asking rate. When the population includes lower-cost types ($\kappa < \Lambda/k$) whose effort saturates at $m^* = k$, the unclipped linear moment decomposition is modified by boundary clipping; numerical optimization across the clipped distribution confirms that higher friction $\lambda_A$ continues to weakly suppress asking, transitioning monotonically from $a^{SE} = 1$ to $a^{SE} = 0$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Non-identification of the two bias channels in the interior regime]
\label{cor:mu-lambda}
On the interior branch ($\bar Q < 0$), defining the normalized excess friction per unit of altruism $\tilde\rho \equiv \frac{\lambda_A - c_Q}{\mu_A} = \frac{b}{\mu_A}$, the optimal pooling rate simplifies to
\[
a^{SE} \;=\; -\,\frac{\bar R_0}{\bar\gamma} \left[ \frac{s - \tilde\rho}{s - 2\tilde\rho} \right],
\]
so $a^{SE}$ depends on the two bias parameters $(\mu_A, \lambda_A)$ strictly through the single scalar ratio $\tilde\rho = (\lambda_A - c_Q)/\mu_A$ (holding primitives $s$ and $\bar R_0/\bar\gamma$ fixed). Consequently,
\[
\frac{\partial a^{SE}/\partial\mu_A}{\partial a^{SE}/\partial\lambda_A} \;=\; -\,\frac{\lambda_A - c_Q}{\mu_A} \;=\; -\,\tilde\rho,
\]
and any two parameter pairs on the same ray $(\lambda_A - c_Q)/\mu_A = \text{const}$ produce the identical pooling ask rate. A leader that discounts downstream welfare ($\mu_A < 1$, $\lambda_A = c_Q \implies \tilde\rho = 0$) cannot be distinguished, from $a^{SE}$ alone, from any leader along the corresponding ray of excess friction.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Scope and structure of Corollary~\ref{cor:mu-lambda}]
This ray-invariance arises because the welfare discount $\mu_A$ scales the user's entire objective $U$ in the leader formulation $\Pi = \mu_A U - (\lambda_A - c_Q)a(k-m)$. If the provider instead discounted only downstream task loss $L$ while internalizing user interaction costs $c_Q$ at full weight, $\mu_A$ and $\lambda_A$ would enter through independent marginal channels. Furthermore, by Corollary~\ref{cor:corner} below, the \emph{unbiased} pooling optimum is always a corner ($\bar Q \ge 0$), so the interior stationary point describes behavior when friction bias is sufficiently large ($2b > \mu_A s$).
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Ray-invariance extends to the corner regime]\label{cor:corner-ray}
On the corner branch ($\bar Q \ge 0$, or whenever the unconstrained stationary point lies outside $[0,1]$), the optimal policy is chosen by the sign of the corner difference $\Pi(1) - \Pi(0) = \bar L + \bar Q$:
\[
\Pi(1) - \Pi(0) \;=\; \mu_A \left[ s\left(\bar R_0 + \frac{\bar\gamma}{2}\right) - \tilde\rho\,(\bar R_0 + \bar\gamma) \right].
\]
Since $\mu_A > 0$, $\mu_A$ factors out completely, and the sign depends solely on the scalar ratio $\tilde\rho = (\lambda_A - c_Q)/\mu_A$. The critical threshold ratio is
\[
\tilde\rho^{*} \;\equiv\; \frac{s\big(\bar R_0 + \bar\gamma/2\big)}{\bar R_0 + \bar\gamma},
\]
with $a^{SE} = 1$ for $\tilde\rho < \tilde\rho^{*}$ and $a^{SE} = 0$ for $\tilde\rho > \tilde\rho^{*}$. Ray-invariance in the excess friction ratio $\tilde\rho$ therefore holds unconditionally across the entire parameter space, interior and corner regimes alike.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Pooling is a corner solution in the unbiased case]\label{cor:corner}
In the unbiased case ($\mu_A = 1, \lambda_A = c_Q \implies b = 0$), the quadratic coefficient is:
\[
\bar Q \;=\; \frac{s^2\,\bar\gamma}{2} \;=\; \frac{(\Lambda - c_Q)^2\,\mathbb{E}[1/\kappa]}{2} \;\ge\; 0 \quad \text{always}.
\]
The second-order condition for an interior maximum therefore fails: $\Pi(a)$ is weakly convex on $[0,1]$, its stationary point is a local minimum, and the true optimum is always a corner, $a^{SE} \in \{0,1\}$, not an interior compromise. Comparing the two corners directly in unclipped algebra:
\[
\Pi(1) - \Pi(0) \;=\; \bar L + \bar Q \;=\; s\left(\bar R_0 + \frac{\bar\gamma}{2}\right) \;=\; (\Lambda - c_Q)\left[ k - \frac{\Lambda + c_Q}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\kappa}\right] \right],
\]
which yields the formal unclipped threshold $k^* \equiv \frac{\Lambda + c_Q}{2}\mathbb{E}[1/\kappa]$. When follower best responses are physically constrained to the feasible domain $m \le k$, Proposition~\ref{prop:firstbest} implies that asking ($a = 1$) weakly dominates guessing for every individual type in the support, so unbiased pooling under physical feasibility selects $a^{SE} = 1$ unconditionally. Genuine interior trade-offs between asking and guessing at the population level emerge under the exact conjunctive success model, as analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
\end{corollary}

This property proves essential for the comparative analysis in Section~\ref{sec:compare}:
Model I exhibits welfare loss relative to the first best primarily through objective bias, which shifts $a^{SE}$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:bias-direction}) and suppresses the operational clarification rate.

\section{Model II: A Screening (Menu) Policy}
\label{sec:model2}

Now let the leader condition its ask rate on the user's declared specification
level (equivalently, by the revelation principle, offer a menu of bundles that
users self-select into). For tractability and clean analytical exposition, we restrict attention to two types $\kappa_L<\kappa_H$ with population
shares $f_L,f_H$ (the continuum-type extension using the standard Mirrlees technique is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Let the leader choose bundles $(m_L,a_L),(m_H,a_H)$ subject
to
\[
\text{IC}_\kappa:\;\; U(m_\kappa,a_\kappa;\kappa) \ge U(m_{\kappa'},a_{\kappa'};\kappa), \qquad
\text{IR}_\kappa:\;\; U(m_\kappa,a_\kappa;\kappa) \ge \underline{U},
\]
to maximize $f_L\Pi_{\kappa_L}(m_L,a_L)+f_H\Pi_{\kappa_H}(m_H,a_H)$.

\subsection{Single-crossing}
The marginal rate of substitution between $m$ and $a$ along an indifference curve
is $\text{MRS}(m,a;\kappa) = -(\partial U/\partial m) / (\partial U/\partial a)$.
From user utility $U(m,a;\kappa) = V - \Lambda(1-a)(k-m) - \frac{\kappa}{2}m^2 - ac_Q(k-m)$,
we have $\partial U/\partial m = \Lambda(1-a) + ac_Q - \kappa m$ and $\partial U/\partial a = s(k-m)$,
where $s \equiv \Lambda - c_Q > 0$ is the net social gain from asking. Hence,
\[
\text{MRS}(m,a;\kappa) \;=\; \frac{\kappa m - [\Lambda - as]}{s(k-m)}, \qquad \frac{\partial \text{MRS}}{\partial \kappa} \;=\; \frac{m}{s(k-m)} \;>\; 0,
\]
which holds strictly for all interior specification levels $0 < m < k$ under the maintained condition $s > 0$.
Higher-$\kappa$ types therefore have strictly steeper indifference curves in $(m,a)$-space: they require more
compensating ask-service per unit of specification effort given up. This satisfies the standard Spence--Mirrlees
single-crossing condition~\citep{spence1973} and underwrites the screening analysis below.

\subsection{No distortion without bias}

\begin{proposition}[Efficiency survives private information when the leader is unbiased]
\label{prop:nodistortion}
If $\mu_A=1$ and $\lambda_A=c_Q$ (so $\Pi_\kappa \equiv U(\cdot;\kappa)$ for every
$\kappa$), then implementing each type's own first-best bundle,
$(m_\kappa,a_\kappa)=(m^{FB}(\kappa),a^{FB}(\kappa))$ from
Proposition~\ref{prop:firstbest}, is incentive compatible, and no further
mechanism design can do better for the leader.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$(m^{FB}(\kappa),a^{FB}(\kappa))$ maximizes $U(\cdot;\kappa)$ by definition, so for
any $\kappa'\ne\kappa$,
\[
U(m^{FB}(\kappa),a^{FB}(\kappa);\kappa) \;\ge\; U(m^{FB}(\kappa'),a^{FB}(\kappa');\kappa)
\]
holds trivially: no bundle can give type $\kappa$ more utility than its own maximizer.
IC holds with no distortion needed. Since $\Pi_\kappa\equiv U(\cdot;\kappa)$, the leader's payoff
from serving each true type is maximized at that type's own first best, so
this menu is also leader-optimal.
\end{proof}

This result highlights a fundamental distinction between human--AI coordination and classical
adverse selection (such as Baron--Myerson or Laffont--Tirole). In regulatory and auction models,
the principal pays monetary transfers to extract surplus and thus treats informational rents as a pure loss.
Here, by contrast, the leader's objective can coincide directly with user welfare; when it does,
incentive compatibility is costless, and private information incurs no welfare penalty.
Distortion in this setting is therefore a pure signature of objective misalignment,
not of asymmetric information per se.

\begin{remark}[Degeneracy of the Unbiased Menu on the Asking Margin]
\label{rmk:unbiased_menu_degenerate}
Under Proposition~\ref{prop:firstbest}, $a^{FB}(\kappa) = 1$ for every type in the population on the physically feasible domain $m \le k$. The unbiased first-best menu is therefore $(m^{FB}(\kappa), 1) = (\min\{k, c_Q/\kappa\}, 1)$, which offers uniform asking $a=1$ across all types and leaves followers to freely choose their preferred specification effort. This menu does not screen across the asking margin: scalar pooling at $a^{SE} = 1$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:corner}) implements each type's first best identically. Consequently, under linear risk on the feasible domain, the welfare gap between pooling and screening at zero bias is identically zero ($\Pi_{II} - \Pi_I = 0$). A positive instrument-restriction gap at zero bias emerges only in settings where first best itself prescribes different asking policies across types, such as the exact conjunctive success model (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) or under attribute loss heterogeneity.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Distortion under bias}

When $\Pi_\kappa \ne U(\cdot;\kappa)$ (bias present), the leader's preferred bundle per type,
\[
(m_\kappa^{B},a_\kappa^{B}) \;=\; \arg\max_{m,a}\Pi_\kappa(m,a)
\]
(computable in closed form by the same method as Proposition~\ref{prop:firstbest}, with
$\Lambda,c_Q$ replaced by their $\mu_A,\lambda_A$-weighted counterparts), no longer
automatically satisfies IC, because $\Pi_\kappa$ and the user's true $U(\cdot;\kappa)$
now rank bundles differently.

\begin{proposition}[Downward distortion of the high-cost type's service]
\label{prop:distortion}
Assume $\Lambda > c_Q$, under-asking bias ($0 < \lambda_A - c_Q < \mu_A(\Lambda - c_Q)$, so that $c_Q < c_Q^{eff} < \Lambda$), low-cost boundary saturation ($\kappa_L \le c_Q^{eff}/k < \kappa_H$), and task complexity $k > k_{\text{crit}}$ (defined in~\eqref{eq:kcrit}).
Then:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Incentive violation of unconstrained menu:} The unconstrained biased menu $(m_L^B, a_L^B) = (k, 0)$ and $(m_H^B, a_H^B) = (c_Q^{eff}/\kappa_H, 1)$ strictly violates $\text{IC}_L$ if and only if $\kappa_L(k + m_H^B) > 2c_Q$.
\item \textbf{Downward distortion:} Under the optimal incentive-compatible menu, the low-cost type receives no distortion in asking service relative to the leader's unconstrained choice ($a_L^{SB} = a_L^B = 0$), while the high-cost type's ask rate is strictly distorted downward:
\[
a_H^{SB} \;<\; a_H^B \;=\; 1.
\]
\item \textbf{Active constraint regime and comparative statics:} In the moderate-bias regime, $\text{IC}_L$ binds alone while $\text{IR}_H$, $\text{IC}_H$, and $\text{IR}_L$ remain strictly slack. Downward distortion $1 - a_H^{SB}$ intensifies as type cost heterogeneity narrows ($\kappa_L \to \kappa_H$), until under sufficiently narrow spread or extreme bias, $\text{IC}_H$ becomes co-active with $\text{IC}_L$ and the optimal menu collapses to pooling.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We establish the result in five analytical steps:

\paragraph{Step 1: Unconstrained biased optima.}
The leader objective per type is $\Pi_\kappa(m,a) = \mu_A [V - \Lambda(1-a)(k-m) - \frac{\kappa}{2}m^2 - a c_Q^{eff}(k-m)]$, where $c_Q^{eff} \equiv c_Q + (\lambda_A - c_Q)/\mu_A$. Under the maintained condition $0 < \lambda_A - c_Q < \mu_A(\Lambda - c_Q)$, effective asking friction satisfies $c_Q < c_Q^{eff} < \Lambda$.
For type $H$ with $\kappa_H > c_Q^{eff}/k$, because $c_Q^{eff} < \Lambda$, $\Pi_{\kappa_H}$ is strictly increasing in $a$ for any $m < k$ (slope $\mu_A(\Lambda - c_Q^{eff})(k-m) > 0$). The leader's unconstrained optimum for type $H$ therefore sets $a_H^B = 1$ and interior specification $m_H^B = c_Q^{eff}/\kappa_H < k$.
For type $L$ with $\kappa_L \le c_Q^{eff}/k$, the partial derivative with respect to effort satisfies $\partial \Pi_{\kappa_L}/\partial m = \mu_A[\Lambda(1-a) + a c_Q^{eff} - \kappa_L m] \ge \mu_A[c_Q^{eff} - \kappa_L k] \ge 0$ for all $m \in [0, k]$ and all $a \in [0, 1]$. Hence $\Pi_{\kappa_L}$ is non-decreasing in $m$, and attains its global maximum on $[0, k] \times [0, 1]$ at $m_L^B = k$. At $m_L^B = k$, zero attributes remain unspecified ($k - m_L^B = 0$), so asking is payoff-irrelevant to both the user and the leader ($\Pi_{\kappa_L}(k, a) = \mu_A[V - \frac{\kappa_L}{2}k^2]$ for all $a$). Setting $a_L^B = 0$ is optimal because it gives zero asking service, minimizing the temptation of type $H$ to mimic type $L$.

\paragraph{Step 2: Upward temptation to mimic ($\text{IC}_L$ violation).}
Evaluating type $\kappa_L$'s payoff from mimicking the high type's bundle $(m_H^B, 1)$ versus accepting its own bundle $(k, 0)$:
\[
U(m_H^B, 1; \kappa_L) - U(k, 0; \kappa_L) \;=\; \left[ V - c_Q(k - m_H^B) - \frac{\kappa_L}{2}(m_H^B)^2 \right] \;-\; \left[ V - \frac{\kappa_L}{2}k^2 \right].
\]
Factoring terms:
\[
U(m_H^B, 1; \kappa_L) - U(k, 0; \kappa_L) \;=\; (k - m_H^B)\left[ \frac{\kappa_L(k + m_H^B)}{2} - c_Q \right].
\]
Because $m_H^B < k$, this difference is strictly positive if and only if $\kappa_L(k + m_H^B) > 2c_Q$. When this condition holds, type $\kappa_L$ strictly prefers the high type's asking contract over completing all attributes itself, violating $\text{IC}_L$.

\paragraph{Step 3: Slackness of participation ($\text{IR}_H$).}
In classical adverse selection with monetary transfers (Baron--Myerson, Laffont--Tirole), the principal's payoff is linearly reduced by agent rent, driving the inefficient agent's $\text{IR}$ to bind at reservation $\underline{U}$.
Here, the principal's payoff $\Pi_{\kappa_H}$ directly incorporates $\mu_A U(\cdot;\kappa_H)$. Depressing $U_H$ directly penalizes the leader's own objective. Thus, the leader adjusts the bundle $(m_H, a_H)$ only as far as necessary to restore $\text{IC}_L$, leaving $\text{IR}_H$ strictly slack: $U(m_H^{SB}, a_H^{SB}; \kappa_H) > \underline{U}$.

\paragraph{Step 4: Global optimization along the $\text{IC}_L$ boundary.}
When $\text{IC}_L$ binds, any feasible allocation must satisfy $U(m_H, a_H; \kappa_L) \le U(m_L, a_L; \kappa_L)$.
Consider the low-cost bundle $(m_L, a_L)$. Because $(m_L^B, a_L^B) = (k, 0)$ maximizes the leader's objective $\Pi_{\kappa_L}$ on the feasible domain, setting $(m_L^{SB}, a_L^{SB}) = (k, 0)$ maximizes surplus on type $L$ while fixing type $L$'s utility at its reservation level $U_L^0 \equiv U(k, 0; \kappa_L) = V - \frac{\kappa_L}{2}k^2$.
To implement this direct mechanism when the follower chooses specification effort $m$, the assistant specifies an off-menu deterrence schedule:
\[
a(m) \;=\; \begin{cases}
a_H^{SB}, & \text{if } m = m_H^{SB}, \\
a_L^{SB} = 0, & \text{if } m = m_L^{SB} = k, \\
0, & \text{if } m \notin \{m_L^{SB}, m_H^{SB}\}.
\end{cases}
\]
Any deviation to an off-menu choice $m \notin \{m_L^{SB}, m_H^{SB}\}$ receives zero clarification ($a=0$) and is strictly dominated for both types.

The entire burden of satisfying $\text{IC}_L$ must therefore fall on the high-cost bundle $(m_H, a_H)$. Along the binding boundary $U(m_H, a_H; \kappa_L) = U_L^0$, utility is strictly linear in $a_H$:
\[
V - \Lambda(k - m_H) + a_H s(k - m_H) - \frac{\kappa_L}{2}m_H^2 \;=\; U_L^0,
\]
where $s \equiv \Lambda - c_Q > 0$. Because $s(k - m_H) > 0$ for all $m_H < k$, this uniquely determines the required asking rate $a_H$ as a function of specification effort $m_H$:
\[
a_H(m_H)(k - m_H) \;=\; \frac{1}{s}\left[ U_L^0 - V + \Lambda(k - m_H) + \frac{\kappa_L}{2}m_H^2 \right],
\]
or equivalently,
\[
a_H(m_H) \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{U(m_H, 1; \kappa_L) - U_L^0}{s(k - m_H)}.
\]
Next, evaluate the leader's payoff $\Pi_{\kappa_H}(m_H, a_H) = \mu_A U(m_H, a_H; \kappa_H) - b a_H (k - m_H)$ along this boundary, where $b \equiv \lambda_A - c_Q > 0$. Using the rent identity $U(m_H, a_H; \kappa_H) = U_L^0 - \frac{\kappa_H - \kappa_L}{2}m_H^2$ and substituting $a_H(m_H)(k - m_H)$, the leader's reduced objective becomes an exact function of $m_H$ alone:
\[
\Pi_{\kappa_H}(m_H) \;=\; \mu_A \left[ U_L^0 - \frac{\kappa_H - \kappa_L}{2}m_H^2 \right] \;-\; \frac{b}{s}\left[ U_L^0 - V + \Lambda(k - m_H) + \frac{\kappa_L}{2}m_H^2 \right].
\]
Expanding terms:
\[
\Pi_{\kappa_H}(m_H) \;=\; \left(\mu_A - \frac{b}{s}\right) U_L^0 \;+\; \frac{b}{s}(V - \Lambda k) \;+\; \frac{b\Lambda}{s}m_H \;-\; \frac{1}{2}\left[ \mu_A(\kappa_H - \kappa_L) + \frac{b}{s}\kappa_L \right] m_H^2.
\]
The second derivative with respect to $m_H$ is:
\[
\frac{d^2 \Pi_{\kappa_H}}{dm_H^2} \;=\; -\left[ \mu_A(\kappa_H - \kappa_L) + \frac{b}{s}\kappa_L \right] \;=\; -\,\frac{\mu_A s(\kappa_H - \kappa_L) + b\kappa_L}{s} \;<\; 0,
\]
which is strictly negative everywhere on $[0, k]$ because $\kappa_H > \kappa_L$, $\mu_A > 0$, $s > 0$, and $b > 0$. Therefore, along the binding $\text{IC}_L$ boundary, the leader's objective is globally strictly concave in $m_H$, with unique interior maximizer:
\[
m_H^* \;=\; \frac{b\Lambda}{\mu_A s(\kappa_H - \kappa_L) + b\kappa_L} \;=\; \frac{\Lambda \tilde\rho}{s(\kappa_H - \kappa_L) + \tilde\rho\kappa_L},
\]
where $\tilde\rho \equiv b/\mu_A = (\lambda_A - c_Q)/\mu_A$.

Evaluating the asking rate at this global boundary optimum:
\[
a_H^{SB} \;=\; a_H(m_H^*) \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{U(m_H^*, 1; \kappa_L) - U_L^0}{s(k - m_H^*)}.
\]
Because $s(k - m_H^*) > 0$ for all $m_H^* < k$, strict downward distortion $a_H^{SB} < 1$ holds if and only if $U(m_H^*, 1; \kappa_L) - U_L^0 > 0$. Expanding this difference with $U_L^0 = V - \frac{\kappa_L}{2}k^2$:
\[
U(m_H^*, 1; \kappa_L) - U_L^0 \;=\; (k - m_H^*)\left[ \frac{\kappa_L(k + m_H^*)}{2} - c_Q \right],
\]
which is strictly positive if and only if:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kcrit}
k \;>\; k_{\text{crit}}(\kappa_L, \kappa_H, \Lambda, c_Q, \lambda_A, \mu_A) \;\equiv\; \frac{2 c_Q}{\kappa_L} - m_H^*.
\end{equation}
Whenever $k > k_{\text{crit}}$, $U(m_H^*, 1; \kappa_L) > U_L^0$ holds strictly, which guarantees:
\[
a_H^{SB} \;<\; a_H^B \;\le\; a_H^{FB} \;=\; 1.
\]
In our representative calibration ($k=10, \Lambda=5, c_Q=2, s=3, b=2, \mu_A=1, \kappa_L=0.3, \kappa_H=0.5$), $c_Q^{eff} = 4.0$, so $\kappa_L = 0.3 \le c_Q^{eff}/k = 0.4 < \kappa_H = 0.5$. The boundary formula applies, giving $m_H^* = 25/3 \approx 8.33$ and $k_{\text{crit}} = \frac{4}{0.3} - \frac{25}{3} = 5.0 < k = 10$. The condition is satisfied with a 100\% margin, producing $a_H^{SB} = 0.75 < 1$, exactly matching the numerical second-best contract.

We now analytically demonstrate that maintaining $a_H = 1$ is globally strictly suboptimal.
Because $c_Q^{eff} < \Lambda$ (which is $\mu_A s - b > 0$), the leader's payoff $\Pi_{\kappa_H}(m_H, a_H) = \mu_A U(m_H, a_H; \kappa_H) - b a_H (k - m_H)$ is strictly increasing in $a_H$ for any fixed $m_H < k$:
\[
\frac{\partial \Pi_{\kappa_H}}{\partial a_H} \;=\; (\mu_A s - b)(k - m_H) \;>\; 0.
\]
For any fixed $m_H$, the leader maximizes surplus within the $\text{IC}_L$-feasible set $\mathcal{F}_H = \{ (m_H, a_H) \in [0, k] \times [0, 1] : U(m_H, a_H; \kappa_L) \le U_L^0 \}$ by setting $a_H$ at its upper bound: $a_H = \min\{1, a_H(m_H)\}$.
Along the locus where this bound binds at $a_H = a_H(m_H)$, the leader's reduced objective $\Pi_{\kappa_H}(m_H)$ is strictly concave in $m_H$ on $[0, k]$ with unique global maximizer $m_H^*$.
Because $k > k_{\text{crit}}$, we have $a_H(m_H^*) < 1$.
Now consider any candidate contract $(\tilde m_H, 1) \in \mathcal{F}_H$ that maintains $a_H = 1$.
Feasibility under $\text{IC}_L$ requires $U(\tilde m_H, 1; \kappa_L) \le U_L^0$, which is equivalent to $a_H(\tilde m_H) \ge 1$.
Because $a_H(m_H^*) < 1$, any such candidate must have $\tilde m_H \ne m_H^*$.
By the strict concavity of $\Pi_{\kappa_H}(m_H)$, it follows that:
\[
\Pi_{\kappa_H}(\tilde m_H, 1) \;\le\; \Pi_{\kappa_H}(\tilde m_H, a_H(\tilde m_H)) \;<\; \Pi_{\kappa_H}(m_H^*, a_H(m_H^*)) \;=\; \Pi_{\kappa_H}(m_H^{SB}, a_H^{SB}).
\]
In our representative calibration, the best contract on the boundary $a_H = 1$ requires $\tilde m_H = k = 10$, yielding leader payoff $\Pi_{\kappa_H}(10, 1) = 75.0$, whereas the interior contract $(8.33, 0.75)$ delivers $\Pi_{\kappa_H}^{SB} = 75.5556$, an exact surplus gap of $5/9 > 0$.
Hence, maintaining $a_H = 1$ is globally strictly suboptimal, and the high-cost type's asking service is strictly distorted downward: $a_H^{SB} < a_H^B \le 1$.

\paragraph{Step 5: Comparative statics of distortion and co-activity of $\text{IC}_H$.}
Evaluating the magnitude of downward distortion:
\[
1 - a_H^{SB} \;=\; \frac{\frac{\kappa_L(k + m_H^*)}{2} - c_Q}{s}.
\]
As type heterogeneity narrows ($\kappa_L$ approaches $\kappa_H$), type $L$'s utility from completing $k$ attributes decreases, increasing type $L$'s temptation to mimic the asking contract. To maintain separation, the leader must distort $a_H^{SB}$ further downward (lower $a_H$).
Furthermore, as bias $\lambda_A$ increases, $m_H^*$ rises toward $k$, which also increases $\kappa_L(k + m_H^*)/2$ and forces $a_H^{SB}$ progressively lower.
When $a_H^{SB}$ approaches $a_L^{SB} = 0$, the high-cost type receives negligible clarification assistance relative to its higher specification cost. Eventually, as friction approaches the task stake ($c_Q^{eff} \to \Lambda$), the leader's marginal gain from asking evaporates. In the prohibitive friction regime ($c_Q^{eff} \ge \Lambda$, or $\lambda_A - c_Q \ge \mu_A(\Lambda - c_Q)$), the leader unconstrainedly prefers zero asking for all types ($\partial\Pi/\partial a \le 0$), shutting down clarification entirely ($a_L = a_H = 0$). In this shutdown regime, both types choose $m=k$, receiving the identical bundle $(k, 0)$, so both $\text{IC}_L$ and $\text{IC}_H$ hold with equality ($0 = 0$). This boundary co-activity reflects uniform clarification shutdown due to prohibitive friction, whereas genuine adverse-selection screening distortion ($a_H^{SB} < a_H^B = 1$) operates strictly in the cost-effective asking regime $c_Q < c_Q^{eff} < \Lambda$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Active Constraint Regimes and Clarification Shutdown]
\label{rmk:active-constraints}
In classical transfer-based screening (such as Baron--Myerson), $\text{IC}_L$ and $\text{IR}_H$ typically bind while $\text{IC}_H$ and $\text{IR}_L$ remain slack. In our setting, solving the full four-variable constrained problem reveals three distinct operational regimes:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Small bias regime ($c_Q^{eff} \approx c_Q$):} When asking friction is close to true cost, the temptation of type $L$ to mimic the asking bundle is weak or absent ($k \le k_{\text{crit}}$), yielding negligible or zero distortion ($a_H^{SB} \approx a_H^B = 1$) with constraints slack.
\item \emph{Moderate bias regime ($c_Q < c_Q^{eff} < \Lambda$ with $k > k_{\text{crit}}$):} Type $L$ faces a strict temptation to mimic, and $\text{IC}_L$ binds alone while $\text{IR}_H$, $\text{IC}_H$, and $\text{IR}_L$ remain strictly slack. The leader directly penalizes itself by depressing follower utility, which keeps participation constraints slack and forces downward distortion $a_H^{SB} < a_H^B = 1$ purely to satisfy $\text{IC}_L$.
\item \emph{Prohibitive friction regime ($c_Q^{eff} \ge \Lambda$, equivalently $\lambda_A - c_Q \ge \mu_A(\Lambda - c_Q)$):} Asking friction exceeds the entire task risk resolved, so the leader unconstrainedly prefers zero asking for all types ($\partial\Pi/\partial a \le 0$). Clarification is uniformly shut down: $a_L = a_H = 0$ and $m_L = m_H = k$. Both types receive the identical bundle $(k, 0)$, so $\text{IC}_L$ and $\text{IC}_H$ hold with equality trivially. This boundary co-activity reflects technological shutdown, not an adverse-selection screening distortion.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Directionality of Policy Misalignment]
\label{rmk:bias-direction}
The downward distortion in Proposition~\ref{prop:distortion} is specific to the under-asking bias case ($0 < \lambda_A - c_Q < \mu_A(\Lambda - c_Q)$), which reflects the empirical reality of turn-penalizing satisfaction metrics. Under an over-asking bias ($\lambda_A < c_Q$), the direction of distortion can reverse, as the leader would favor excessive clarification.
\end{remark}

\section{Comparing the Two Regimes}
\label{sec:compare}

\begin{corollary}[Menus weakly dominate pooling]
\label{cor:dominance}
The leader's optimal expected payoff under Model II is weakly greater than under
Model I: any pooling policy $a$ is a feasible (degenerate) menu with
$a_L=a_H=a$, trivially satisfying IC, so the unconstrained menu optimum is at
least as good.
\end{corollary}

Beyond payoff dominance, the central comparison lies in the structural decomposition
of welfare losses across the two regimes, enabled by Propositions~\ref{prop:aSE}--\ref{prop:distortion}:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Model I (pooling):} Under the linear-risk specification on the physically feasible domain $m \in [0, k]$, an unbiased assistant selects $a^{SE} = 1$, which implements the first-best policy $a^{FB}(\kappa) = 1$ for every type in the population (Proposition~\ref{prop:firstbest}). Consequently, in the absence of bias ($\lambda_A = c_Q$), the pooling restriction incurs zero welfare loss ($\Pi_{II} - \Pi_I = 0$). Welfare loss under pooling arises when objective bias is present ($\lambda_A > c_Q$), which suppresses the single ask rate $a^{SE}$ below the socially efficient level (Corollary~\ref{cor:bias-direction}), or in the exact conjunctive model (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), where first-best policies differ across types ($a^{FB} \in \{0, 1\}$) and a uniform rate cannot simultaneously tailor to heterogeneous needs.
\item \textbf{Model II (screening)} incurs welfare loss from bias \emph{alone}
(Proposition~\ref{prop:nodistortion}): in the absence of bias, screening recovers full
efficiency despite private cost information. Under bias, the loss takes the form of the
downward distortion characterized in Proposition~\ref{prop:distortion}.
This represents a fundamentally different mechanism from the instrument-restriction loss of pooling: it stems
not from an inability to condition on observed effort, but from the requirement to
prevent opportunistic mimicking across types.
\end{itemize}

\section{Testable Predictions}
\label{sec:predictions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item[\textbf{P1}] (Prop.~\ref{prop:accuracy}, Cor.~\ref{cor:mono}) Holding the
assistant's policy fixed, higher guessing accuracy is associated with
shorter specifications alongside weakly higher user welfare, and never with
an overall welfare decline despite the reduction in prompt length.
\item[\textbf{P1$'$}] (Model I remark) Holding accuracy fixed, the effect of a
higher \emph{ask rate} on specification length flips sign depending on
whether $c_Q \gtrless \Lambda=L(1-g)$: where clarifying questions are inexpensive
relative to the risk they resolve, users specify less as the ask rate rises
(substitution); where questions are relatively costly to answer, users specify
\emph{more} (pre-emption). This prediction operates within the model and is distinct
from P1's accuracy channel.
\item[\textbf{P2}] (Prop.~\ref{prop:bias}) The correlation between turn-level
satisfaction and eventual task success weakens or inverts specifically for
under-asking assistants, and can be benchmarked against the derived $a^{SE}$
of Proposition~\ref{prop:aSE} rather than an assumed ask rate.
\item[\textbf{P3}] Making ask-rate statistics visible to users shifts
specification length toward the sophisticated best response analyzed in
Section~\ref{sec:naive-soph}.
\item[\textbf{P4}] (Prop.~\ref{prop:distortion}) Ask rates should rise with a
user's revealed cost of providing detail (terseness) when clarification policies are screening menus or under nonlinear success models with attribute heterogeneity, rather than responding exclusively to model confidence.
\item[\textbf{P5}] Users with lower self-reported error-catching rates exhibit
declining specification effort across an interactive session (following Section~\ref{sec:sources}'s dynamic channel).
\item[\textbf{P6}] (Cor.~\ref{cor:corner}, Prop.~\ref{prop:distortion}) Under an assistant with asking friction bias ($\lambda_A > c_Q$), a pooling policy suppresses the single ask rate ($a^{SE} < 1$), which disproportionately depresses the welfare of high-cost (terse) users who rely most heavily on clarification. While a menu-screening policy also distorts high-cost asking downward ($a_H^{SB} < 1$) to preserve incentive compatibility, screening preserves higher total welfare by tailoring specification expectations to user capabilities ($\Pi_{II} \ge \Pi_I$). In settings characterized by nonlinear exact success or heterogeneous attribute stakes (where first best prescribes asking only for terse users), an unbiased pooling policy also incurs a structural instrument-restriction loss by forcing uniform asking.
\item[\textbf{P7}] (Prop.~\ref{prop:distortion}) Even when an assistant can
condition on declared specification effort, terse users receive fewer clarifying questions
than the assistant's unconstrained per-type preference would prescribe ($a_H^{SB} < a_H^B$). This downward distortion arises
specifically from the incentive-compatibility constraint $\text{IC}_L$; it intensifies as user cost types become closer ($\kappa_L \to \kappa_H$), requiring deeper distortion to prevent mimicry until separation is no longer viable, and diminishes as cost heterogeneity widens ($\kappa_H \gg \kappa_L$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Empirical Strategy and Experimental Design}
\label{sec:empirical}

The behavioral predictions P1--P7 (including P1$'$) translate our contract-theoretic mechanisms into observable margins for experimental testing. In naturalistic software engineering logs, however, assistant policy bias $\lambda_A$ and user specification costs $\kappa$ are typically confounded with task difficulty and user experience. To causally test our predictions, we propose a between-subjects randomized controlled trial on software developers performing standardized implementation tasks:

\paragraph{Experimental design and treatments.}
Participants are randomly assigned to interact with code assistants operating under distinct clarification regimes:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Treatment A (Low-asking policy, $a = 0.1$):} The assistant aggressively guesses ambiguous attributes with minimal clarification.
\item \emph{Treatment B (High-asking policy, $a = 0.8$):} The assistant frequently asks targeted clarifying questions before generating code.
\item \emph{Treatment C (Menu-conditioned screening policy):} The assistant dynamically selects its clarification rate based on the structural completeness of the initial user prompt, implementing the screening menu derived in Section~\ref{sec:model2}.
\item \emph{Control (Unbiased benchmark):} The assistant's clarification rate is calibrated to the user-optimal benchmark.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Measurement and identification.}
For each programming session, instrumentation logs: (i) upstream prompt length and specified invariants\slash edge cases ($m$); (ii) clarification turn count ($a(k-m)$); (iii) downstream test suite execution and correctness; and (iv) post-session subjective developer satisfaction.
By exogenously varying the clarification policy $a$, this protocol directly tests whether users economize on prompt specification in response to anticipated assistance, distinguishes the substitution channel ($\Lambda > c_Q$) from the pre-emption channel ($c_Q > \Lambda$, Prediction P1$'$), and isolates the welfare losses attributable to objective bias versus pooling restrictions (Predictions P6 and P7).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The linear-risk approximation to the conjunctive success function is the main
tractability assumption carried through Sections~\ref{sec:stackelberg}--\ref{sec:compare}.
To verify that this simplification does not drive our core theoretical findings, we
evaluate both the unbiased pooling optimum and the biased screening menu directly under the exact conjunctive payoff $q(a,g)^{k-m}$
(independently of the linear-risk closed forms, via direct numerical grid search and
constrained optimization). Across wide ranges of task complexity ($k\in[3,20]$) and guessing accuracy ($g\in[0.50,0.95]$), numerical optimization confirms that the qualitative structure survives under the exact nonlinear specification (\path{underspec_sim/verifications/verify_exact_conjunctive.py}, recorded in \path{outputs/exact_conjunctive_robustness.csv}):
the corner-solution property of Corollary~\ref{cor:corner} persists ($a^{SE}_{\text{exact}} \in \{0, 1\}$); downward distortion ($a_H^{SB} < a_H^B$) persists robustly, with exceptions concentrated at small $k$ where specification effort saturates at $m=k$; and participation constraints remain strictly slack. Furthermore, numerical solutions of the full four-variable
constrained menu problem under the exact conjunctive payoff across $\lambda_A\in[c_Q,10c_Q]$ and $\mu_A\in[0.1,1.0]$ confirm that $\text{IC}_H$ binds alongside $\text{IC}_L$ under extreme bias (\path{underspec_sim/verifications/verify_exact_bias_sweep.py}, recorded in \path{outputs/exact_bias_sweep.csv} and Figure~\path{outputs/exact_bias_sweep.png}). This reproduces the three-regime structure (no binding constraint, $\text{IC}_L$ alone, and $\text{IC}_L+\text{IC}_H$ together) identified under the linear-risk approximation, with only the boundary between moderate- and extreme-bias regimes shifting slightly (from $\lambda_A\approx6.0$ to $\lambda_A\approx8.0$ at $\mu_A=1.0$). The extreme-bias reversal is therefore an intrinsic structural feature of the screening contract space, rather than an artifact of the linear-risk approximation.

Regarding the regularity condition (Assumption~\ref{ass:reg}), monotonicity of prompt specification ($m^*(g)$ weakly decreasing in $g$) holds whenever $(k-m)\ln q \ge -1$. While monotonicity can fail when the cross-partial turns strictly positive, empirical monotonicity can still survive locally in low-stakes regimes as a boundary property (\path{underspec_sim/verifications/verify_monotonicity_survival.py}, recorded in \path{outputs/monotonicity_survival.csv}), because Topkis submodularity is a sufficient rather than necessary condition for comparative statics.

A stylized modeling assumption is attribute symmetry ($k$ binary attributes with identical loss $L$ and guessing probability $g$). In practical software engineering, attributes exhibit substantial loss heterogeneity: omitting an architectural invariant or security convention causes severe failure ($L_i \gg 0$), while omitting a stylistic naming convention carries negligible cost ($L_j \approx 0$). In an extension with attribute-specific losses $L_i$, the user's objective becomes $U = V \prod_i q_i - c(m) - \sum_i a_i c_Q$. Ordering attributes by descending risk stake $\Lambda_i = L_i(1-g_i)$, users rationally specify the highest-stakes attributes first, while assistants prioritize asking on the highest-residual-stake attributes. The fundamental contract-theoretic mechanism, namely the substitution between upstream specification effort and automated clarification under misaligned asking friction, generalizes directly to the heterogeneous-loss setting.

A related limitation concerns the empirical identification of $\lambda_A$. Every
bias-driven prediction (Corollary~\ref{cor:bias-direction}, Proposition~\ref{prop:distortion}, and
predictions P2, P6, P7) is stated in terms of under-asking bias ($\lambda_A > c_Q$), but backing out
$\lambda_A$ from observed policy alone is circular. Corollary~\ref{cor:mu-lambda}
formalizes this as an exact non-identification result on the interior branch: $a^{SE}$ depends on
$(\mu_A,\lambda_A)$ only through their ratio, so a discounting-driven leader and a
friction-driven leader with the identical excess friction ratio $(\lambda_A - c_Q)/\mu_A$ cannot be distinguished
from pooling behavior alone. An independent empirical handle, such as an explicit penalty
on turn count in the training objective or reward specification, would be needed before
the bias-versus-heterogeneity predictions in Section~\ref{sec:predictions} can be tested
without circularity. The two-type restriction in Model II avoids
ironing complications at the cost of generality; a continuum
formulation using the standard Mirrlees technique is a natural next step to characterize
the continuous distribution of downward distortions across arbitrary cost distributions. We also assume truthful revelation of specified attributes (abstracting from
cheap-talk ambiguity within what is communicated) and a single leader. Modeling competition
among multiple assistants represents an appealing extension, as market competition
could plausibly discipline asking bias directly.

A broader methodological conclusion concerns the limitations of applying classical contract-theoretic intuition directly to human--AI settings without accounting for non-monetary, partially aligned incentives. In classical mechanism design (such as Baron--Myerson or Laffont--Tirole), the principal pays monetary transfers to extract surplus, and agent informational rent directly reduces the principal's payoff. In human--AI interaction, the assistant's objective directly incorporates user welfare. As a consequence, incentive compatibility is costless in the absence of bias (Proposition~\ref{prop:nodistortion}), participation constraints do not bind at the bottom of the type distribution, and unbiased pooling naturally induces corner solutions rather than interior compromises (Corollary~\ref{cor:corner}). Economic analyses of automated assistance must therefore explicitly incorporate the non-monetary, partially aligned nature of the interaction rather than relying on direct analogies to cash-transfer mechanisms.
